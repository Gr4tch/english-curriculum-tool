> **TEXTBOOK PRINTED MAIN IDEA (p. 256):** American leaders met at the Constitutional Convention to debate and decide how to reform the national government.

### Official Section Vocabulary
- **delegate** *(Content Vocabulary, p. 256)*: a person chosen or elected to represent a group of people
- **dual sovereignty** *(Content Vocabulary, p. 257)*: the concept that state governments have certain powers the federal government cannot overrule
- **federalism** *(Content Vocabulary, p. 257)*: the support of a government where power is shared between the central, national government and that of states or provinces
- **Framers** *(Content Vocabulary, p. 257)*: delegates to the 1787 Constitutional Convention who helped shape the content and structure of the U.S. Constitution

### Section Reading Passage (By Subheading)

#### 1. DELEGATES GATHER (p. 256)
**[¶1]** By 1787, the United States was growing geographically and economically. It was clear the Articles of Confederation were not able to address complications from that growth. Robert Morris of Pennsylvania and Alexander Hamilton of New York believed the country needed a stronger government in order to deal with its problems.

**[¶2]** Leaders called for a meeting to discuss reform. The meeting, held in Philadelphia on May 25, 1787, was called the Constitutional Convention . Every state sent delegates , or representatives, except Rhode Island. The tiny state did not trust the concept of a powerful federal government, which it feared would be the outcome of the Constitutional Convention.

#### 3. GOALS AND CHALLENGES (p. 257)
**[¶1]** The delegates debated for four months. Because of their experience, the Framers aimed to create a government that was neither too strong, like the British monarchy, nor too weak, as under the Articles of Confederation. The delegates wanted national, state, and local governments to share governing power, an idea called federalism .

**[¶2]** They also felt dual sovereignty , or giving state governments certain powers that the national government could not overrule, was important. Delegates at the Convention agreed upon other main principles. One was republicanism. In a republican government, citizens elect officials to represent them, and these officials must act and govern according to the law.

**[¶3]** Another principle that guided the delegates was that elections and legislative votes would be decided by majority rule. Majority rule requires that the candidates or legislation receive at least one more than one-half of all votes, even when there are more than two choices. Majority rule means that support for a successful candidate or law must be strong.

**[¶4]** Convention delegates supported the idea of federalism but debated how to divide state-federal power. They disagreed about how much power state and national governments should have and about how much authority they should grant the three branches of the federal government. Madison and other Framers were influenced by the writing of the French political philosopher Baron Charles de Montesquieu .

**[¶5]** Montesquieu developed the idea of the separation of powers within a government. He felt that power should be divided equally among three distinct branches that would be responsible for making laws, enforcing laws, and interpreting laws. Each branch would have the ability to limit the power of the other two through a series of checks and balances .

**[¶6]** This way, no one group could gain too much power.
